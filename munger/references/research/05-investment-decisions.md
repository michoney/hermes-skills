### The 1973-74 Crash: Critical Context

An investment of $1,000 on January 1, 1973, would have shrunk to $467 by January 1, 1975 -- a 53% peak-to-trough drawdown. At end of 1974, Blue Chip Stamps comprised 61% of the portfolio.

Munger wound up Wheeler, Munger, and Co. in 1976. This was not a voluntary retirement from a winning streak -- the partnership was effectively destroyed by concentrated positions in a severe bear market.

**Key insight:** The 1973-74 experience is the crucible that shaped Munger's later emphasis on QUALITY as the precondition for concentration. Early Munger concentrated in statistically cheap businesses (closer to Graham/cigar-butt style). Post-crash Munger concentrated only in wide-moat, high-quality businesses. The framework evolved not from theory but from near-catastrophic loss.

**Source:** GuruFocus, Yahoo Finance "Lessons From Charlie Munger's Early Partnership" [second-hand]

## 2. Specific Investments Made and Advocated

### 2.1 See's Candies (1972) -- The Framework-Changing Investment

**What happened:** Berkshire Hathaway acquired See's Candies in 1972 for $25 million. At the time, Buffett was reluctant -- the price was above what Graham-style valuation would support.

**Munger's role:** Munger persuaded Buffett to pay up for quality. This was the first time Munger successfully pushed Buffett away from cigar-butt investing toward quality businesses at fair prices.

**Results:** Cumulative pre-tax earnings exceeded $2 billion from the original $25 million investment. See's generated cash that funded other Berkshire acquisitions.

**The pricing power lesson:** Munger and Buffett discovered they could raise prices 10% per year and no one cared. "Learning that changed Berkshire and was really important."

> Buffett credited See's with educating him: "If we hadn't bought See's, we wouldn't have bought Coke. So thank See's for the $12 billion." [first-hand, Buffett about Munger's influence]

**Why this matters for the skill:** See's Candies is the empirical foundation of Munger's "quality at fair price" framework. It proved that brand loyalty + pricing power + low capital intensity = compounding machine. Every subsequent Berkshire quality investment (Coca-Cola, GEICO, Apple) traces its intellectual lineage to See's.

**Source:** 25iq "A Dozen Things Buffett and Munger Learned from See's Candies," Fortune, SBO Financial [mix of first-hand Buffett/Munger quotes and second-hand analysis]

### 2.2 Costco -- Munger's Personal Conviction Investment

**Timeline:** First invested ~1997. Served on Costco's board from 1997 until death in 2023. Owned ~$100 million personal position.

**The thesis:**
- Sol Price's membership model: customers prepay for access, merchandise sold near cost, membership fees fund operations
- Strict self-imposed discipline of passing purchasing power advantages through to customers (ethical commitment to value)
- Virtuous cycle: lower prices -> higher volume -> economies of scale -> lower prices again
- Low-margin, high-loyalty model that competitors could not replicate without destroying their own higher-margin structures

> "I love everything about Costco. I'm a total addict, and I'm never going to sell a share." -- 2023 Daily Journal meeting [first-hand]
> Munger called it "a business that became the best in the world in its category." [first-hand]

**Couldn't convince Buffett:** Munger was notably unable to convince Buffett to invest Berkshire's capital in Costco, despite decades of advocacy. This is a rare case where the two disagreed on a major holding.

**The "find Costcos" philosophy:**
> "The secret is to find Costcos, not good exits." Munger's wealth came from "relentless holding and not slick sell signals." [first-hand, per Benzinga/Yahoo Finance reporting]

**Source:** CNBC, Benzinga, Financhill, Medium analysis [mix of first-hand Munger quotes and second-hand analysis]

### 2.3 BYD -- The Single Best Berkshire Investment (Munger's Claim)

**Timeline:** Berkshire invested $230 million in 2008 for 225 million shares (~10% stake). Began selling in 2022. Fully exited by Q3 2025.

**How he found it:** Through Li Lu, founder of Himalaya Capital. Munger trusted Li Lu's judgment on Chinese companies.

**The thesis:** BYD was not a speculative EV startup but a vertically integrated battery and vehicle manufacturer with extraordinary engineering leadership.

> Munger called BYD founder Wang Chuanfu "a combination of Thomas Edison and Jack Welch." [first-hand]
> "I have never helped do anything at Berkshire that was as good as BYD -- and I only did it once." -- 2023 Daily Journal meeting [first-hand]

**Returns:** Approximately 39x over 17 years. Berkshire's initial investment grew to roughly $8-9 billion at peak. Total profit reportedly exceeded $7 billion.

> At BYD's peak share price, Berkshire's stake was worth $9.5 billion. [second-hand, Benzinga]

**Munger on BYD vs. Tesla:**
> "BYD is so far ahead of Tesla in China, it's almost ridiculous." -- 2023 [first-hand, CNBC]

**Why this matters:** BYD demonstrates Munger's willingness to act decisively when conviction is high, even outside traditional circle of competence (Chinese auto/battery manufacturing). He relied on a trusted intermediary (Li Lu) to extend his circle. This is the POSITIVE example of China investing, in contrast to Alibaba.

**Source:** Benzinga, CNBC, Fortune, SCMP [mix of first-hand quotes and second-hand reporting]

### 2.4 Freddie Mac via Wesco Financial -- The Regulatory Arbitrage

**Timeline:** Late 1980s purchase, sold in 2000.

**The investment:** Wesco's subsidiary Mutual Savings bought 28.8 million shares of Freddie Mac for $72 million. At the time, only thrifts could buy Freddie Mac stock, and Munger took the maximum position allowed by law.

**Returns:** Sold in 2000 for approximately $1.4 billion. Roughly 20x pretax, netting $852 million after taxes. Approximately 28% compounded annual return over 11-12 years.

**Strategic context:** While the entire savings and loan industry was collapsing around him, Munger's decision to diversify Wesco away from pure thrift operations and into financial assets (including the Freddie Mac stake) effectively saved the company from the S&L disaster.

**Why this matters:** Shows Munger's ability to exploit structural/regulatory inefficiencies (only thrifts could buy Freddie Mac) combined with business quality assessment. He saw Freddie Mac's economics clearly while competitors were too distracted by the S&L crisis to act.

**Source:** Hedge Fund Alpha, GuruFocus, Circle of Competence blog [second-hand, compiled from Wesco shareholder letters]

### 2.5 Daily Journal Portfolio -- Extreme Concentration in Practice

**Timeline:** First portfolio filing Q4 2013. Positions held essentially unchanged until Munger's death and beyond.

**Final holdings (as of Q4 2025):**

| Stock | Weight | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| Wells Fargo | 47.60% | Bought at ~$8/share during financial crisis |
| Bank of America | 39.76% | Post-crisis opportunity |
| Alibaba | 10.33% | The mistake (see below) |
| US Bancorp | 2.30% | Smallest position |

Total portfolio: ~$276.65 million. Turnover rate: 0.0% (no changes in recent periods, even after Munger's death).

**Key details:** Munger reportedly spent ~$20 million of Daily Journal's excess cash on several bank stocks during the financial crisis, buying into extreme fear. He then held for over a decade without touching the positions.

**What this demonstrates:** Munger's extreme concentration philosophy in action -- 97% of the portfolio in three stocks. Also demonstrates "sit on your ass investing" -- literally zero turnover for years. The bank purchases during the financial crisis show his willingness to buy when others are panicking.

**Source:** ValuSider, StockCircle, 13Radar, 247WallSt [second-hand, compiled from 13F filings]

### 2.6 Munger's Personal Portfolio -- Three Holdings

> "The Munger family has three stocks: a block of Berkshire, a block of Costco, and a block of Li Lu's fund. The rest is dribs and drabs." [first-hand, per multiple interview reports]

> "What are the chances that Costco's going to fail? What are the chances that Berkshire Hathaway's going to fail? What are the chances that Li Lu's portfolio in China is going to fail? The chances that any one of those things happening is almost zero." [first-hand]

> "If you're not a know-nothing investor, if you're actually capable of figuring out something that will work better, you're just hurting yourself looking for 50 when three will suffice. Hell, one will suffice if you do it right." [first-hand]

## 3. Investments He REJECTED (The Too Hard Pile in Practice)

### 3.1 Technology Stocks (Pre-2016)

Berkshire avoided tech stocks for decades because Buffett and Munger considered them outside their circle of competence. They doubted that even considerable effort studying the industry would materially improve their ability to evaluate these businesses. So they passed.

**The evolution:** By the late 2010s, Munger acknowledged the world had changed:
> "Everybody needs some significant participation in the 12 companies that do better than everybody else." [first-hand, Acquired podcast]
> "I think that the modern investor, to get ahead, almost has to get in a few stocks that are way above average." [first-hand]

This represents a meaningful shift -- from "tech is too hard" to "tech is now too important to ignore."

### 3.2 Airlines

Munger and Buffett consistently put airlines in the Too Hard pile.

> "Can unions have an impact?" and "Does the structure of the airline industry" matter? -- Munger's checklist questions about airline investments [first-hand]

Buffett's characterization (which Munger endorsed): the airline business "grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the growth, and then earns little or no money."

**Note:** Buffett eventually broke this rule with a brief airline investment in 2016-2020, which he then sold at a significant loss during COVID-19. Munger's original instinct was vindicated.

### 3.3 Cryptocurrency -- Not "Too Hard" but "Evil"

This is not a Too Hard classification -- Munger actively condemned crypto as "disgusting and contrary to the interests of civilization." He even praised China for banning it.

> "Bitcoin is disgusting and contrary to the interests of civilization." [first-hand]
> "Crazy, stupid gambling." [first-hand]
> "I admire the Chinese. I think they made the correct decision." [first-hand]

**Analytical note:** Munger's crypto position is unusual because it's moral/emotional rather than analytical. He didn't say "I can't evaluate it" (Too Hard) or "it's overvalued" (analytical). He said it's "evil." This is one of the clearest cases where Munger's disliking/hating tendency may have overwhelmed his otherwise analytical framework. The Chinese research source flags this as "selective rationality."

### 3.4 SPACs and Venture Capital

> "The world would be better off without [SPACs]." [first-hand]
> On venture capital: "They're not great investors -- they're not great at anything. To hell with them!" [first-hand]

## 4. Investments He Got WRONG and What He Learned

### 4.1 Dexter Shoes (1993) -- The Moat That Wasn't

**What happened:** Berkshire acquired Dexter Shoe for $434 million, paying with 25,203 shares of Berkshire stock. The business went to zero.

**Why it failed:** Chinese competitors came up fast, charging half the price. Customers switched. "What I had assessed as durable competitive advantage vanished within a few years." (Buffett, but Munger was equally culpable)

**The double mistake:** Not only did they misjudge the moat, they paid with Berkshire stock. Those 25,203 shares were worth over $6 billion by end of 2016.

**Lesson learned:** The Dexter failure is the empirical foundation of Munger's insistence on MOAT DURABILITY as the core quality filter. It's not enough to identify a moat -- you must assess how durable it is against competitive entry, particularly from low-cost international competitors. This directly reinforced the shift from cigar-butt (cheap price) to quality (durable advantage).

**Source:** Novel Investor, 25iq, Benzinga [mix of first-hand Buffett/Munger quotes and second-hand analysis]

### 4.2 Alibaba -- The Most Recent Public Failure

**What happened:** Daily Journal built a significant position in Alibaba. The stock declined substantially as Chinese regulatory crackdowns hit the company.

**Munger's own analysis of the failure:**
> "I regard Alibaba as one of the biggest mistakes I ever made. In thinking about Alibaba, I got charmed by their position in the Chinese internet; I didn't stop to realize they're still a goddamn retailer." -- Daily Journal 2023 meeting [first-hand]

**What went wrong (multi-layered):**
1. **Circle of competence violation:** Munger didn't adequately understand Chinese regulatory dynamics
2. **Mere association tendency:** His positive view of China generally (BYD success, admiration for Chinese governance) colored his assessment of Alibaba-specific risks
3. **Business model misunderstanding:** He treated it as a dominant internet platform when it was fundamentally a retailer with thin margins
4. **Doubled down after decline:** Classic commitment/consistency bias -- added to the position as it fell rather than re-evaluating the thesis
5. **Jack Ma's political misstep:** Munger noted Ma made "mistakes in dealing with the Chinese government" -- a risk that was foreseeable given the political environment

**Contradiction flagged:** Munger called Alibaba "a goddamn retailer" dismissively, yet Costco -- his favorite investment -- is also a retailer. The difference is structural (Costco's membership model + cost discipline vs. Alibaba's platform-dependent revenue + regulatory exposure), but the surface-level inconsistency in his framing is notable.

**Ironic post-mortem (flagged from Chinese research):** Alibaba stock rose 74% after Munger's death. The "goddamn retailer" was surging. This doesn't prove Munger was wrong long-term, but it complicates the narrative.

**Source:** Benzinga, Piranha Profits, TheStreet, Yahoo Finance, Daily Journal meeting transcripts [first-hand Munger quotes + second-hand analysis]

### 4.3 Wheeler Munger 1973-74 Concentration Blow-Up

See Section 1 above. Not a specific stock mistake but a portfolio construction mistake: excessive concentration (61% in Blue Chip Stamps) without adequate quality assessment of the concentrated position.

### 4.4 Errors of Omission -- What He Missed

Munger considered errors of omission Berkshire's biggest investing mistakes:

> "The most extreme mistakes in Berkshire's history have been mistakes of omission." [first-hand]

**Google:** Berkshire had direct operational evidence of Google's economics -- GEICO was paying for clicks that cost Google essentially nothing to provide. They saw the evidence and failed to act.

> Munger: "I would say that we failed you there... we were probably smart enough" to figure out Google. [first-hand]
> Munger said he was "ashamed" of missing Google. [first-hand, per Bloomberg/Acquirer's Multiple]

**Walmart:** Referenced as a missed opportunity, though specific financial details are less documented.

**Amazon:** Munger gave himself a partial pass on Amazon, calling Bezos "a miracle worker" -- implying the outcome was harder to predict.

**Belridge Oil:** During the 2001 Berkshire meeting, Munger described a personal omission that cost him $200 million -- he was offered 300 shares of Belridge Oil, then offered 1,500 more three days later but would have had to sell something else to buy them.

**Source:** Yahoo Finance, Bloomberg, 25iq, Acquirer's Multiple, CNBC [mix of first-hand quotes and second-hand reporting]

### 4.5 Department Stores

Munger and Buffett bought Hochschild, Kohn department store chain "at a discount to book and liquidating value. It didn't work." [first-hand, Munger]

This reinforced the lesson from Dexter: statistical cheapness without business quality is a losing strategy.

## 5. Behavior During Market Crashes

### 5.1 The 2008-2009 Financial Crisis

**What he said:**
- Munger and Buffett sensed something dangerous as early as 2007
- Munger advocated keeping substantial liquidity before the crash

**What he did:**
- Berkshire deployed tens of billions during the crisis, including a $5 billion investment in Goldman Sachs (preferred stock with warrants -- structured to minimize downside while capturing upside)
- At Daily Journal, Munger steered the company's first stock investments into the market, buying Wells Fargo at ~$8/share, Bank of America, and US Bancorp during maximum fear
- The Daily Journal bank purchases were made with the company's excess cash -- he was deploying capital when others were hoarding it

**Key insight:** Munger didn't try to time the bottom. He bought during extreme fear because the businesses were cheap relative to intrinsic value. The margin of safety was enormous at crisis prices for well-capitalized banks.

**Source:** Yahoo Finance/GuruFocus (Buffett and Munger called the 2008 crisis), ValuSider/13Radar (Daily Journal filings) [second-hand compilation]

### 5.2 The 2020 COVID Crash

**What he said:**
- Called it the worst situation since World War II
- Cautioned about the extreme uncertainty

**What he did:**
- Berkshire sold $30 million worth of stocks but did NOT buy the dip
- Waited with substantial liquidity through the storm
- Notably more cautious than in 2008 -- did not deploy aggressively

> "He was watching and waiting, preferring to go through the storm with a lot of liquidity as he saw the situation as very risky." [second-hand, CCN]

**Contrast with 2008:** In 2008, Munger deployed aggressively. In 2020, he stayed on the sidelines. The difference may be:
1. Age and conservatism (96 years old in 2020 vs. 84 in 2008)
2. Valuation starting point (2020 markets were more expensive pre-crash than 2007 markets)
3. Nature of the crisis (financial crisis = analyzable systemic risk vs. pandemic = unknowable biological risk outside circle of competence)

### 5.3 The Philosophy of Crash Behavior

> "If you're not willing to react with equanimity to a market price decline of 50%, two or three times a century, you're not fit to be a common shareholder." [first-hand, multiple venues]

> "It's not supposed to be easy. Anyone who finds it easy is stupid." [first-hand]

Munger's crash behavior is defined by what he DOESN'T do: he doesn't panic sell, doesn't try to time bottoms, doesn't leverage into falling markets. He holds quality positions through drawdowns and occasionally buys more when conviction is high and prices are extreme.

## 6. Position Sizing: Concentration vs. Diversification IN PRACTICE

### The Philosophy

> "The idea of diversification makes sense to a point if you don't know what you're doing." [first-hand]
> "The whole secret of investment is to find places where it's safe and wise to non-diversify." [first-hand]
> He called conventional diversification wisdom "balderdash -- a safety net for people who didn't have an edge." [first-hand]

### How He Actually Sized Positions

**Personal portfolio:** Three holdings (Berkshire, Costco, Li Lu's fund) -- effectively each holding is 25-40% of personal wealth.

**Daily Journal:** Four stocks, with top two (Wells Fargo + Bank of America) comprising ~87% of the portfolio. This is extreme concentration by any institutional standard.

**Wheeler, Munger & Co.:** Blue Chip Stamps was 61% of the portfolio at end of 1974. Five positions dominated the partnership.

**Berkshire (with Buffett):** "If Buffett were running $50, $100, $200 million, he would have 80 percent in five positions, with 25 percent for the largest." [first-hand, Munger describing the ideal]

### The Risk Management Nuance

Despite advocating concentration, Munger acknowledged:
- "A diversified portfolio is right for many investors even though that's not how I invested." [first-hand]
- He used checklists, insisted on margin of safety, pre-defined kill criteria
- He diversified across BUSINESS MODELS and CASH FLOW DRIVERS rather than just ticker count
- Concentration was ALWAYS conditional on genuine understanding -- circle of competence was the prerequisite

### Match Size to Conviction

The sizing hierarchy in practice:
1. **Largest positions:** Where moat + valuation + stewardship are ALL strongest (Berkshire, Costco)
2. **Meaningful positions:** Where thesis is strong but one element is slightly weaker (bank stocks)
3. **Smaller positions:** At the edge of circle of competence (Alibaba -- and this is where he lost)
4. **Zero position:** Outside circle of competence -- no amount of upside justifies it

## 7. Evolution from Cigar-Butt to Quality Investing

### The Before: Graham-Style Value Investing (1960s-early 1970s)

Munger's early investing at Wheeler, Munger & Co. was closer to Benjamin Graham's cigar-butt approach:
- Buy stocks trading below net current asset value
- Emphasize "margin of safety" as price discount
- Focus on statistical cheapness without adequate weight on business quality
- Take profits when price reaches estimated value

**Evidence:** Blue Chip Stamps, Hochschild Kohn department stores, early concentrated positions that were cheap but not high-quality.

### The Catalyst: See's Candies (1972) + 1973-74 Crash

Two events transformed Munger's framework:

1. **See's Candies (1972):** Proved that a quality business with pricing power could compound value indefinitely. The $25 million investment generated over $2 billion in cumulative pre-tax earnings. This was not a cigar butt -- it was a permanently compounding machine.

2. **1973-74 bear market:** Demonstrated that concentration in statistically cheap businesses without quality moats leads to devastating drawdowns. The near-destruction of Wheeler, Munger proved that cheap price alone is insufficient protection.

### What Specifically Changed in the Framework

| Dimension | Before (Cigar Butt) | After (Quality) |
|-----------|---------------------|------------------|
| Primary filter | Price vs. liquidation value | Business quality and moat durability |
| Margin of safety | Discount to asset value | Competitive position + pricing power + management quality |
| Holding period | Until price reaches value | Indefinitely ("sit on your ass") |
| Position sizing | Concentrated in cheap names | Concentrated in quality names |
| Capital allocation | Buy cheap, sell at value, repeat | Buy quality, hold, let compounding work |
| Sell trigger | Price reaches estimated value | Thesis breaks (business deteriorates) |
| Scalability | Doesn't scale (requires finding new cheap stocks) | Scales with growing businesses |

### The Formulation

> "Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices." -- Munger to Buffett [first-hand, per Buffett's repeated telling]

### Why the Transition Was Necessary (Beyond Philosophy)

The practical reason was scale. Cigar-butt investing works for small capital bases -- buying up dirt-cheap businesses and liquidating or turning them around. Once Berkshire's capital base grew, the approach became physically impossible. You can't deploy $10 billion into net-net stocks. But you CAN deploy $10 billion into Coca-Cola, Apple, or See's Candies-type businesses.

Munger saw this scaling constraint earlier than Buffett and pushed the transition. The intellectual framework (quality > cheapness) was validated by See's Candies. The practical necessity (Berkshire's growing capital base) made the transition inevitable.

**Source:** Fortune, Yahoo Finance, GuruFocus, Investors Podcast, Quartr [second-hand analysis grounded in first-hand quotes]

## 9. Contradictions and Unresolved Tensions

### Tension 1: China Admiration vs. China Investment Losses

Munger publicly praised China's governance, praised the ban on cryptocurrency, invested successfully in BYD, yet lost significantly on Alibaba due to the very regulatory unpredictability he seemed to excuse. His post-hoc rationalization ("they're still a goddamn retailer") may mask the real failure: political risk assessment in a system he admired too much to analyze objectively.

### Tension 2: "Errors of Omission Are Worst" vs. Concentration Philosophy

If errors of omission are your worst mistakes (missing Google, Walmart, Amazon), the logical response is to cast a wider net -- which contradicts concentration. Munger's resolution: "we were probably smart enough to figure out Google" -- meaning the omission wasn't from lack of circle expansion, but from laziness or inattention within the existing circle. The solution isn't diversification but better vigilance.

### Tension 3: "React with Equanimity to 50% Drawdown" vs. 2020 Inaction

In 2008, Munger bought aggressively during the crash (consistent with his philosophy). In 2020, he sat on the sidelines despite a severe crash. If equanimity means buying when others panic, 2020 was inconsistent. Possible explanations: age, COVID uncertainty being genuinely outside circle of competence, or the crash being too brief to deploy at scale.

### Tension 4: "Sit on Your Ass" vs. BYD Exit

Munger advocated holding great businesses indefinitely. But Berkshire began selling BYD in 2022 and fully exited by Q3 2025. If BYD was "the best thing I ever helped do at Berkshire," why sell? Possible explanations: portfolio rebalancing needs at Berkshire level, geopolitical risk reassessment post-COVID, or Buffett's different risk tolerance on China exposure post-Munger's influence.

### Tension 5: Alibaba "Goddamn Retailer" vs. Costco Also Being a Retailer

Munger dismissed Alibaba as "still a goddamn retailer" while his favorite investment was Costco -- also a retailer. The distinction is structural (Costco's membership model, cost discipline, and U.S. regulatory environment vs. Alibaba's platform model and Chinese political exposure), but the framing is inconsistent. The real issue with Alibaba was likely regulatory/political risk, not the retail label.
