## 1. Wheeler, Munger Partnership (1962-1975): The Formative Crucible

### Performance by Phase

| Period | Wheeler, Munger (Annual) | Dow Jones (Annual) | Notes |
|--------|--------------------------|-------------------|-------|
| 1962-1969 | 37.1% | ~6-7% | Spectacular outperformance |
| 1962-1970 | 24.3% | 6.4% | Still strong after 1970 pullback |
| 1970-1972 | 13.9% | 12.2% | Barely beat the Dow |
| 1973 | -31.9% | -13.1% | Severe underperformance |
| 1974 | -31.5% | -23.1% | Another brutal year |
| Full 1962-1975 | 19.8% | 5.0% | Long-term outperformance despite drawdowns |

### What He Did
- Ran a concentrated portfolio focused on deep value and special situations
- **Did NOT reduce exposure** ahead of the 1973-74 crash despite elevated valuations
- Suffered a peak-to-trough drawdown of ~53% (worse than the index)
- $1,000 invested Jan 1, 1973 became $467 by Jan 1, 1975 (vs. $688 for the Dow)
- Partnership net asset value fell to just $7 million by end of 1974
- **Wound down the partnership in 1976** --- this was not a voluntary tactical exit but an acknowledgment that the vehicle was broken

### Key Insight for the Skill
Munger's first major test under real market stress was a FAILURE of risk management. He compounded 19.8% annually over the full period, but the 1973-74 experience left deep scars. His later emphasis on quality over cheapness and avoiding leverage can be traced directly to living through a 53% drawdown in a concentrated, leveraged-adjacent structure.

## 2. Dot-Com Bubble (1999-2000): Patient Contrarianism Vindicated

### What He Said
> "The Internet bubble circa 2000 is the most extreme in modern capitalism. In the 1930s, we had the worst depression in 600 years. Today is almost as extreme in the opposite way."

> "If you mix raisins with turds, they're still turds."

### What He Did
- **Stayed completely out of tech/internet stocks** through the entire bubble. Zero exposure.
- Berkshire underperformed the S&P 500 by more than 40% in 1999 at the height of the bubble
- Endured enormous social and media pressure. Berkshire was widely mocked; critics said Buffett and Munger were dinosaurs.
- **Did not capitulate or style-drift** --- maintained discipline through 18+ months of underperformance
- From 2000-2002 after the bubble burst, Berkshire outperformed the S&P 500 by more than 60%
- Wesco Financial's portfolio was concentrated in old-economy names (Freddie Mac at $1.9B was the largest holding by 1999)

### Was He Right About Timing?
**Yes on direction, imperfect on timing.** He was early---Berkshire took significant relative pain in 1998-99 before being vindicated. The key lesson: Munger's framework correctly identified the bubble but could not predict when it would burst. His response was to simply not participate rather than try to time the exit.

### Key Insight for the Skill
Munger's approach to bubbles is avoidance, not timing. He doesn't try to ride the bubble and exit before the crash. He simply refuses to participate. This means he will always underperform in the late stages of a mania but will preserve capital through the crash. The cost is sustained relative underperformance during euphoria.

## 3. The Freddie Mac Trade (1988-2000): Brilliant Entry, Prescient Exit

### What He Did
- Through Wesco's subsidiary Mutual Savings, bought 28.8 million shares of Freddie Mac for $72 million in 1988. This was the maximum allowed by law for a thrift.
- By 1999, the position was worth $1.9 billion (a ~26x return)
- **Sold the entire position in 2000**, generating $852.4 million in after-tax securities gains for Wesco
- Freddie Mac subsequently collapsed in the 2008 financial crisis and was placed into government conservatorship

### Why This Matters
This is one of the most under-discussed Munger trades. He bought a financial company he understood deeply (leveraging his thrift subsidiary's structural advantage), held for 12 years through massive appreciation, and exited **before** the business deteriorated. The sale in 2000 was years before Freddie Mac's problems became apparent, suggesting Munger may have recognized early signs of governance or risk-taking deterioration.

### Key Insight for the Skill
Munger CAN sell. Despite his "sit on your ass" reputation, when he loses confidence in the business quality, he exits. The Freddie Mac sale is the counter-example to the Wells Fargo hold---it shows Munger's framework at its best, where deep understanding of the business led to a timely exit.

## 4. 2008 Global Financial Crisis: Aggressive, Contrarian, and Correct

### What He Did (Through Daily Journal Corporation)

This is arguably Munger's finest hour as an independent investor:

- **Deployed 71% of Daily Journal's cash** into bank stocks when most investors were fleeing the sector
- Bought Wells Fargo at $8 per share (describing rock-bottom prices as "a once-in-forty-years opportunity")
- Invested approximately $20 million total, with the vast majority in bank stocks at generational lows
- Key positions: Bank of America, Wells Fargo, US Bancorp, POSCO (Korean steelmaker)
- **Held every single position for over a decade** --- the 2013 first portfolio filing showed the same names, and as late as 2023 the same quantities remained

### The Scale of the Return
By 2013, those investments had more than tripled the company's value. The $20 million deployed at crisis lows grew to be worth multiples of the original investment.

### What He Said
At Berkshire, Buffett and Munger were also aggressive:
- Berkshire invested $5 billion in Goldman Sachs (preferred stock + warrants) in September 2008
- Invested $3 billion in General Electric
- Invested $6.5 billion in Wrigley/Mars deal
- Later invested $5 billion in Bank of America (2011, during the European debt crisis aftershock)

### Key Insight for the Skill
Munger's crisis behavior reveals his true risk model: he views price declines in fundamentally sound businesses as REDUCED risk, not increased risk. When everyone else sees danger (falling prices), Munger sees safety (lower entry points). But critically, he was selective---he bought bank stocks specifically because he understood the businesses and believed they would survive. He didn't buy indiscriminately.

The prerequisite for his crisis aggression was having cash BEFORE the crisis. Munger didn't need to sell anything to buy---Daily Journal had ample liquidity. This "dry powder" discipline is an essential but often overlooked element of his approach.

## 5. 2020 COVID Crash: Cautious, Passive, and (Arguably) Too Slow

### What He Did
- **Berkshire did NOT aggressively buy** during the March 2020 crash, in contrast to 2008
- Berkshire sold its entire airline portfolio (Delta, Southwest, American, United) at a significant loss---approximately $6 billion of value
- Berkshire sat on a record $137 billion in cash through the recovery
- Daily Journal did NOT make significant new investments during the COVID crash

### What He Said
- "I don't think we'll have a long-lasting Great Depression. I think government will be so active that we won't have one like that. But we may have a different kind of a mess. All this money-printing may start bothering us."
- Noted that companies were "frozen" and not seeking capital: "People are frozen... their playbook doesn't have this as a possibility."
- Later warned that unprecedented Federal Reserve intervention had driven markets to "dangerously high levels"

### Why He Didn't Buy Aggressively
Several factors explain the contrast with 2008:
1. **Speed of the crash and recovery**: The March 2020 crash was extremely fast (34% decline in 23 trading days) and the recovery was equally fast. By the time analysis was complete, prices had already rebounded.
2. **Unprecedented nature**: COVID was genuinely novel---unlike 2008 (a financial crisis Munger understood), this was a pandemic with no clear analog.
3. **Age factor**: Munger was 96 in 2020. While not incapacitated, the urgency and energy required for crisis investing may have been diminished.
4. **Berkshire's size**: At $500B+ in assets, deploying capital meaningfully required enormous positions that were harder to find.

### The Airline Sale: A Mistake?
Buffett admitted selling airlines was "an understandable mistake." The airlines subsequently recovered and appreciated significantly. This is notable because it shows that even Munger/Buffett's framework can produce incorrect sell decisions during unprecedented events. The "too hard" categorization of COVID led them to exit rather than hold through uncertainty.

### Key Insight for the Skill
COVID 2020 reveals the limitations of Munger's crisis playbook. His framework works best when the crisis is FINANCIAL (overvalued assets correcting, bank runs on solvent institutions) and less well when the crisis is EXOGENOUS (pandemic, war, natural disaster). The framework's strength---deep business understanding---becomes a weakness when the variable destroying value is entirely outside the business domain.

## 6. 2021-22 Bubble & Burst: Cassandra on SPACs, Crypto, Meme Stocks

### What He Said

**On SPACs**: "I think the world would be better off without them" and called the speculation "the sign of an irritating bubble."

**On Meme Stocks (GameStop/WallStreetBets)**: "That's the kind of thing that can happen when you get a whole lot of people who are using liquid stock markets to gamble the way they would in betting on racehorses... It must end badly, but I don't know when."

**On Crypto**: Called Bitcoin "a turd" and trading crypto "just dementia." In May 2021: "Of course I hate the bitcoin success." After FTX collapse (Nov 2022): Called crypto "partly fraud and partly delusion" and "I'm not proud of my country for allowing crypto sh-t."

### What He Did
- **Did NOT participate** in any SPAC, meme stock, or crypto activity
- **DID buy Alibaba** starting Q1 2021---which is ironic because his own speculative bubble warnings could have applied to the valuation frenzy in Chinese tech
- Built Alibaba position from 165,000 shares (Q1 2021) to 602,000 shares (Q4 2021) even as China's regulatory crackdown intensified
- Reduced to 300,000 shares in Q1 2022 after massive losses

### The Alibaba Contradiction
Munger was simultaneously:
1. Warning about bubbles, excess, and speculation
2. Doubling down on a Chinese tech stock during a regulatory crackdown
3. Using leverage to do so (which he admitted contradicted his own philosophy)

This is perhaps the most revealing inconsistency in Munger's late career. His macro-level diagnosis (bubble everywhere) was correct, but his micro-level action (buying Alibaba aggressively) was wrong. The explanation may be that Munger saw Alibaba as "value" (cheap on earnings) while dismissing SPACs and crypto as "speculation"---but the distinction broke down because the cheapness was a trap driven by political risk.

## 7. China/BYD: The Crown Jewel Investment

### Timeline of Actions

| Date | Action | Details |
|------|--------|---------|
| 2008 (Sep 29) | Initial purchase | Berkshire Hathaway Energy bought 225M shares of BYD at HK$8/share for $230M |
| 2008-2022 | Hold | No sales for 14 years despite multiple BYD drawdowns |
| Aug 2022 | Begin trimming | Berkshire starts reducing position |
| Feb 2023 | Munger's assessment | "I have never helped do anything at Berkshire that was as good as BYD" |
| Nov 2023 | Munger passes away | At age 99; BYD stake remained one of Berkshire's most profitable |
| By mid-2024 | ~76% sold | Berkshire had sold about 76% of its holding |
| Sep 2025 | Full exit | Berkshire completely exits BYD after 17 years |

### Investment Thesis
- **Li Lu introduced BYD to Munger** via Himalaya Capital. Li Lu had invested in BYD for Munger's family portfolio first.
- Munger saw BYD not as an EV startup but as **a vertically integrated battery and manufacturing company** with extraordinary leadership
- Called CEO Wang Chuanfu "a combination of Thomas Edison and Jack Welch"---specifically citing his hands-on engineering expertise
- The thesis was: genius CEO + vertical integration + massive addressable market (batteries, EVs, energy storage) = asymmetric upside

### Performance
- $230M initial investment grew to a peak of ~$9.5B (roughly 40x return)
- Even after trimming, Berkshire earned more than **$7 billion in profit**
- By Feb 2023: "At the current price of BYD stock, a little BYD is worth more than the entire Mercedes corporation"

### BYD During Drawdowns
Munger held BYD through multiple significant drawdowns:
- 2011-2012: BYD stock fell ~80% from its 2009 peak as the EV market slowed and government subsidies were cut
- 2015-2016: Chinese market crash took BYD down significantly
- 2018: Trade war fears hit Chinese stocks broadly
- **Through every drawdown, Munger held the full position.** He did not trim, did not waver, did not express doubt.

### Munger's China Thesis (Broader)
- "The Chinese economy had better prospects over the next 20 years than almost any other big economy"
- "I could get much better companies at much lower prices" in China
- Downplayed Taiwan invasion risk: "The Chinese leader is a very smart, practical person"
- Called US-China tensions "stupid, stupid, stupid"
- Cited Apple as model for how Sino-American relations should work

### The BYD vs. Alibaba Contrast
What made BYD work and Alibaba fail reveals Munger's actual edge:

| Factor | BYD | Alibaba |
|--------|-----|---------|
| Source of thesis | Li Lu (deep China expert, personal trust) | Munger's own analysis |
| Business type | Manufacturing (tangible, analyzable) | Platform/retail (network effects, harder to value) |
| Regulatory risk | Government was PROMOTING EVs | Government was CRACKING DOWN on tech platforms |
| CEO assessment | Spent time understanding Wang Chuanfu's engineering | Admitted he "didn't stop to realize they're still a retailer" |
| Position sizing | Large but single entry over 14 years | Aggressive doubling down during decline |
| Leverage | None | Admitted using leverage |

**Key Insight**: Munger's framework works best when he has deep understanding of the product/manufacturing process, a trusted intermediary with local expertise, and aligned government policy. It fails when he relies on narrative ("Chinese internet dominance") without ground-level understanding.

## 8. Macro Views: Does He Use Macro at All?

### Official Position: Ignore Macro
> "Like the weather, I just ignore [macroeconomic trends]."

Munger's stated approach is bottom-up, business-by-business. He does not model GDP, interest rates, or inflation into investment decisions.

### Actual Behavior: Macro Awareness Without Macro Trading

Despite the "ignore macro" rhetoric, Munger's actual behavior shows significant macro awareness:

**On Inflation**:
- Called inflation "the biggest long-range danger we have probably, apart from nuclear war"
- "Inflation is a very serious subject. You can argue it's the way democracies die."
- "If you try and print too much money, it eventually causes terrible trouble"
- Predicted the post-2020 inflation surge: warned that money-printing would "start bothering us"
- Practical advice: "I think the safe assumption for an investor is that over the next hundred years, the currency is going to zero"

**On Interest Rates**:
- "I've never believed there was a perfect correlation between interest rates and inflation... it's complex and not easily quantifiable"
- Warned that modern politicians won't permit Volcker-style tightening: "You may wish you had a Volcker-style recession instead of what you're going to get"

**On Government Policy**:
- Deeply skeptical of excessive government spending and money-printing
- Warned about the consequences of fiscal excess long before it became consensus

### The Paradox
Munger says he ignores macro but:
1. His crisis buying (2008) was timed to a macro event (financial crisis)
2. His Freddie Mac sale (2000) preceded a macro event (housing bubble)
3. His COVID caution was driven by macro uncertainty
4. His inflation warnings proved prescient
5. His China thesis was fundamentally a macro bet on economic development

**Resolution**: Munger doesn't trade macro, but he uses macro awareness as a RISK FILTER. He doesn't buy or sell based on GDP forecasts, but he avoids situations where macro deterioration could cause permanent capital loss and gets aggressive when macro panic creates mispriced assets.

## 9. Holding Through Drawdowns: Revealed Risk Tolerance

### Documented Drawdown Tolerance

| Vehicle | Event | Drawdown | Munger's Action |
|---------|-------|----------|----------------|
| Wheeler, Munger | 1973-74 bear market | ~53% | Held; wound down partnership after |
| Berkshire Hathaway | 1987 crash | ~25% in one day | Held |
| Berkshire Hathaway | 1998-99 underperformance | ~40% relative to S&P | Held; no style drift |
| Berkshire Hathaway | 2008 GFC | ~50% from peak | Bought more (bank stocks) |
| BYD | 2011-12 post-IPO peak decline | ~80% | Held every share |
| Daily Journal | 2022 portfolio decline | ~20%+ | Held (reduced Alibaba partially) |
| Alibaba (Daily Journal) | 2021-2023 | ~40-50%+ | Partially sold; admitted mistake |

### Pattern Analysis

**When Munger holds through drawdowns**:
- Business fundamentals are intact
- The drawdown is price-driven (market panic) not business-driven
- He has deep understanding of the business
- He has a trusted information source

**When Munger sells**:
- Business quality is deteriorating (Freddie Mac in 2000)
- He recognizes his own thesis was wrong (Alibaba partial sale)
- Position sizing has become excessive relative to portfolio
- External circumstances have permanently changed the business (airlines in 2020 via Berkshire)

### Actual Risk Tolerance Profile
Munger's revealed preferences show:
- **Extreme tolerance for price volatility**: Willing to endure 50%+ drawdowns without selling
- **Zero tolerance for permanent capital impairment**: Will sell if the business itself is broken
- **Moderate tolerance for thesis uncertainty**: Will partially sell (Alibaba) when unsure, rather than all-or-nothing
- **Concentration comfort**: Routinely held 30-50% of portfolio in a single position (BYD in Berkshire Energy, Bank of America in Daily Journal)
- **Time horizon measured in decades**: BYD held 14 years before first trim; Wells Fargo held 10+ years; bank stocks from 2008 held to 2023+

## 10. Synthesis: Munger's Market Regime Playbook

### Regime Response Matrix

| Market Regime | Munger's Typical Response | Historical Example |
|---------------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| **Bubble/Mania** | Refuse to participate; hold cash; endure underperformance | 1999-2000 dot-com |
| **Financial Crisis** | Aggressively buy quality assets at distressed prices | 2008 GFC (bank stocks) |
| **Exogenous Shock** | Cautious; wait for clarity; may sell what he can't understand | 2020 COVID (airline sale, no buying) |
| **Bear Market (no crisis)** | Hold through; buy selectively if prices reach "once in X years" levels | 2022 rate hike bear |
| **Speculative Excess** | Verbal condemnation; zero participation; BUT may buy what he considers "value" in the same environment | 2021 (condemned SPACs/crypto while buying Alibaba) |
| **Geopolitical Tension** | Downplay risks; maintain positions; trust in economic fundamentals | China tensions 2018-2023 |

### What the Skill Should Model

1. **Crisis hierarchy**: Financial crises = buy aggressively. Exogenous crises = wait and assess. Speculative manias = sit out entirely.

2. **Cash as strategic weapon**: Munger's crisis buying was only possible because he maintained liquidity BEFORE the crisis. The skill should always consider dry powder adequacy.

3. **Drawdown tolerance calibration**: Munger endured 50%+ drawdowns routinely. The skill should test whether the user/portfolio can actually sustain Munger-level drawdowns before recommending Munger-level concentration.

4. **Sell discipline**: Despite the "hold forever" reputation, Munger sold Freddie Mac (2000), airlines (2020 via Berkshire), and partially sold Alibaba (2022). The sell triggers are: business deterioration, thesis invalidation, and position sizing excess.

5. **Macro as risk filter, not trading signal**: Use macro awareness to identify environments where risk of permanent loss is elevated (excess leverage, political risk, bubble valuations), but don't trade on GDP or rate forecasts.

6. **The Alibaba lesson**: When the thesis relies on narrative rather than deep operational understanding, when the information source is your own analysis rather than a trusted expert with local knowledge, and when you're buying into a declining position with leverage---the framework is at its weakest. The skill should flag these conditions explicitly.
