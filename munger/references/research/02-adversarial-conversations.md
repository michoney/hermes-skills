## I. Times Munger Publicly Disagreed with Buffett (And Who Was Right)

### 1.1 The Fundamental Philosophical Shift: "Fair Business at Great Price" → "Great Business at Fair Price"

**The disagreement:** Buffett's early career (the Ben Graham years) focused on buying cheap, mediocre businesses — "cigar butts" with one puff left. Munger pushed him to abandon this for high-quality businesses at reasonable prices.

**Munger's argument:** *"A great business at a fair price is superior to a fair business at a great price."* [First-hand, multiple attributions]

**Who was right:** Munger. Buffett himself has credited this shift as transformative: *"Charlie shoved me in the direction of not just buying bargains, as Ben Graham had taught me. This was the real impact he had on me."* See's Candies (1972) was the first major application — bought for $25M (3x book value), a price Graham would have rejected. Cumulative pre-tax earnings from See's have exceeded $2 billion.

**Risk officer lesson:** Quality of business > statistical cheapness. Munger's adversarial contribution to Berkshire was killing the cigar-butt strategy.

### 1.2 Costco: Munger's Obsession vs. Buffett's Indifference

**The disagreement:** Munger was a Costco board member and personally invested heavily. Buffett showed minimal interest. At the 2011 Berkshire meeting, Buffett joked that if given a last request before execution, he'd say *"Shoot me first"* rather than listen to Charlie give another Costco speech.

**Munger's argument:** Costco is a perfect moat business: credibility of restraint (11-15% margin cap vs. 25-50% industry), membership renewal above 90%, recession-proof demand. *"Find Costcos, not good exits."* [First-hand, Daily Journal meetings]

**Who was right:** Munger. Costco stock returned ~2,500% from 1997 to 2023. Berkshire never took a major position, which Munger considered one of their missed opportunities.

**Risk officer lesson:** When your most experienced partner is obsessed with something and you ignore it, that itself is a signal worth examining.

### 1.3 Estate Planning: Hold Berkshire vs. Index It

**The disagreement:** Buffett has publicly stated his wife should put 90% of her inheritance in a low-cost S&P 500 index fund after his death. Munger wanted his heirs to hold their Berkshire shares for the higher upside potential.

**Who was right:** Unresolved. Both make defensible arguments. Buffett's position reflects epistemic humility (future Berkshire managers may be worse). Munger's reflects confidence in Berkshire's structural advantages.

**Risk officer lesson:** Even partners who agree on 95% of things can reach different conclusions on the same data. This is healthy, not a flaw.

### 1.4 The Nature of Their Disagreements

Notably, Buffett has said: *"Charlie and I have never had an argument."* When they disagreed, Munger's move was: *"Well, you'll end up agreeing with me because you're smart and I am right."* [First-hand, multiple attributions]

Their disagreement style was adversarial reasoning, not adversarial relationship. Munger was the designated skeptic. When he pushed back, it carried weight precisely because he was usually silent.

## II. How Munger Evaluates Management — Specific Examples and Red Flags

### 2.1 The Three Filters (Management Is the Third)

Munger's investment checklist has three sequential filters:
1. **Can I understand the business?** (Circle of competence)
2. **Does it have a durable competitive advantage?** (Moat)
3. **Does management have integrity and talent?** (Character)

> **"We like the business great first. Then, second, we want a great manager. But we have not made a huge success by investing in great managers who take over lousy businesses."**
> — Daily Journal meeting. [First-hand]

The sequence matters: business quality > management quality. A great manager in a bad business still loses.

### 2.2 Specific Management Red Flags

**Red Flag 1: Compensation Excess**
> *"Perhaps the most important rule in management is 'Get the incentives right.'"* [First-hand]
> *"Those in high-level American business have a moral duty to be underpaid — not to get all that you can, but to actually be underpaid."* [First-hand]
> *"I'd rather throw a viper down my shirt front than hire a compensation consultant."* [First-hand, Berkshire meeting]

When Munger sees management using compensation consultants, he reads it as a signal that they are trying to justify extracting maximum personal value rather than building business value.

**Red Flag 2: CEOs Who Rely on Consultants for Capital Allocation**
Munger observed that CEOs who recognize their lack of capital-allocation skills often turn to their staffs, management consultants, or investment bankers — which "accentuates the capital-allocation problem rather than solving it." [First-hand, attributed via Poor Charlie's Almanack]

**Red Flag 3: The "Denial" Pattern**
> *"If I had to name one factor that dominates human bad decisions, it would be what I call denial. If the truth is unpleasant enough, their mind plays tricks on them and they think it isn't really happening."*
> — Daily Journal meeting. [First-hand]

Applied to management: when a CEO keeps throwing money into an obsolescing business model, that's denial (#11, Psychological Denial from the 25 tendencies).

**Red Flag 4: EBITDA Promotion**
> *"Every time you see the word EBITDA, you should substitute the words 'bullshit earnings'."* [First-hand]

Munger and Buffett said the number of times they'll invest in a company that touts EBITDA would be "about zero."

**Red Flag 5: Excessive Debt + Aggressive Accounting**
When he sees these together, Munger's pattern recognition fires: this is the setup for a blow-up. Companies carry out shenanigans "with caution and restraint — by doing it bit by bit, adding a little bit of fake earnings a year, it will be less likely to be noticed." [First-hand via Poor Charlie's Almanack / "Great Financial Scandal of 2003"]

### 2.3 Specific Management Evaluations

**Valeant's Mike Pearson:**
- *"Valeant is like ITT and Harold Geneen come back to life, only the guy is worse this time."* [First-hand, 2015]
- Munger identified the pattern months before the stock collapsed: serial acquisition + price gouging + accounting games = "phony growth record."
- After the collapse: *"Valeant was a sewer, and those who created it deserved the opprobrium they got."* [First-hand, 2016 Berkshire meeting]

**Jack Ma (Alibaba):**
- *"Jack Ma was very arrogant to tell the Chinese government how dumb they were."* [First-hand, 2021 Daily Journal]
- Separately: *"It's like poking a bear in the nose with a sharp stick. It's not smart."* [First-hand]
- Munger's assessment was management behavioral risk, not business risk. Alibaba's business was fine; Ma's ego triggered regulatory retaliation.

**Wang Chuanfu (BYD):**
- Initial reaction when Wang wanted to enter auto manufacturing: *"God, this is horrible news. It's a really dumb idea."* [First-hand via Li Lu accounts]
- But Munger changed his mind after deeper analysis and held for 15+ years through a 38x return. This is a rare example of Munger admitting his initial management skepticism was wrong.

**Salomon Brothers' John Gutfreund:**
- Gutfreund knew about trading improprieties (submitting bids in customer names without permission) and was urged by Salomon's general counsel to report to federal authorities, who told him *"it's the right thing to do."* Gutfreund delayed.
- Munger drew the lesson: management failures are often failures of speed, not awareness. Gutfreund knew the right thing. He just didn't do it fast enough. This is Tendency #5 (Avoid-Inconsistency) in action — the reluctance to change course once a pattern is established.

**Wells Fargo's John Stumpf:**
- *"Salomon was a 'traffic ticket' that almost brought down a business."* — Munger drew a parallel to Wells Fargo, noting that early management action could have prevented catastrophic damage. [First-hand, 2017 Berkshire meeting]
- Buffett added that the biggest mistake was ignoring communications about the sales scandal and not acting. Munger's framework: the same management pattern (denial + delay) keeps recurring.

### 2.4 Munger's Management Summary Rule

> **"The only duty of corporate executive is to widen the moat. Every day is to widen the moat."** [First-hand]

Any management behavior that does NOT widen the moat is a red flag. Compensation extraction, empire-building acquisitions, financial engineering, regulatory games — all narrow the moat.

## III. Critiques of Other Investors' Mistakes

### 3.1 Bill Ackman and Valeant

Munger's critique was both direct and layered:
- On the investment itself: *"Valeant was a sewer."*
- On Ackman personally: *"He certainly made a brilliant investment in General Growth Properties and he's totally right about Herbalife. He's very articulate and intelligent and he's a fellow Harvard man, so he's not all bad."* [First-hand, 2016 Berkshire meeting]

The pattern Munger identified: Ackman was right on some bets (GGP, Herbalife short thesis) but was seduced by Valeant's "phony growth record" — the same pattern that caught 1960s investors with ITT. Smart investors are not immune to social proof (#15) and commitment/consistency bias (#5).

Ackman later asked Munger publicly to "be nicer about Valeant." Munger did not comply.

### 3.2 The Active Management Industry

> *"Only 5% of money managers have the skill required to consistently beat the index averages after costs."* [First-hand, Daily Journal 2023]

Munger doesn't blame individual managers — he blames the structural incentives:
> *"Investment banking profession will sell s--- as long as s--- can be sold."* [First-hand]

The pattern he sees: the industry's fee structure incentivizes activity, not results. This is pure Tendency #1 (Reward/Punishment Super-Response) — managers are rewarded for gathering assets, not for performance.

### 3.3 The 2008 Financial Crisis Investors/Institutions

> *"A combination of megalomania, stupidity, insanity, and I would say evil on the part of bankers and mortgage brokers."* [First-hand]

Munger's diagnosis was that the crisis was a Lollapalooza (#25) — multiple biases stacking:
- **Incentive bias (#1):** Brokers paid per loan, not per performing loan
- **Social proof (#15):** Everyone else is doing it
- **Denial (#11):** Housing prices always go up
- **Overconfidence (#12 + #13):** Models said the risk was contained

### 3.4 Crypto Investors

Munger's harshest sustained critique was reserved for crypto:
- *"Disgusting and contrary to the interests of civilization."*
- *"Crazy, stupid gambling."*
- *"It's like somebody else is trading turds and you decide, I can't be left out."* — This is a direct diagnosis of social proof (#15) and FOMO.
- *"People who oppose my position are idiots."*

He escalated his language over the years: "rat poison" → "rat poison squared" → "venereal disease" → "crypto sh-t" → "crypto crappo."

### 3.5 The Pattern Munger Sees Across All Investor Mistakes

Three recurring errors:
1. **Ignoring incentive structures** — following people whose interests are not aligned with yours
2. **Chasing phony growth records** — serial acquirers with accounting games (ITT → Valeant → SPACs)
3. **Denial when the thesis breaks** — continuing to hold/add when the original reason is gone

## IV. Accounting Shenanigans and Financial Fraud Detection

### 4.1 "The Great Financial Scandal of 2003"

This fictional piece, distributed at the 2001 Wesco Annual Meeting, describes an imaginary tech company called "Quant Tech" that games its financials:

**Key mechanisms Munger identified:**
- Stock options not counted as compensation expense — *"an amazingly peculiar accounting convention"* chosen because corporate managers preferred it
- The accounting profession went along because of client pressure (Tendency #1, #22 — incentive + authority)
- Earnings built up slowly through incremental fraud — *"bit by bit, adding a little bit of fake earnings a year"*
- The rule-making body FASB stood for *"Financial Accounts Still Bogus"* — Munger's coinage

**Munger's structural critique of auditors:**
> There exist very powerful incentives for accountants and auditors to, on the margin, let their clients get away with some misdemeanors. [First-hand, via Poor Charlie's Almanack]

> A majority of the horrors faced would not have happened if the accounting profession were organized properly. [First-hand]

> The accountancy profession has become a sewer. [First-hand, attributed via multiple sources]

### 4.2 Specific Fraud Detection Heuristics

**Heuristic 1: Follow the Incentives**
If management's compensation is tied to reported earnings, assume those earnings are optimistic. The stronger the incentive, the more suspicious you should be.

**Heuristic 2: EBITDA as a Warning Signal**
Any company that prominently touts EBITDA is trying to hide real costs. Depreciation is real. Amortization is real. Pretending they don't exist is not a sign of good faith.

**Heuristic 3: The "Too Smooth" Earnings Pattern**
Real businesses have lumpy earnings. Consistently smooth growth — especially at round numbers or just above consensus — suggests accounting management.

**Heuristic 4: Beware the Modest Fraudster**
> *"The fraudsters who target him would have modest little offices and modest demeanor."* [First-hand]
Munger warns that the biggest frauds don't look flashy. They look conservative and trustworthy — which is itself a psychological play on Tendency #22 (Authority).

**Heuristic 5: The "Bit by Bit" Accumulation**
> Companies carry out financial shenanigans *"with caution and restraint — by doing it bit by bit, adding a little bit of fake earnings a year, it will be less likely to be noticed and exposed than a large sudden increase."* [First-hand]

This is the boiling frog of fraud: each individual step is too small to trigger alarms.

### 4.3 Derivatives: Special Category of Accounting Fraud Risk

> *"Comparing accounting for derivatives with sewage is an insult to sewage."* [First-hand]

Munger's concern: derivatives create earnings on paper (mark-to-model) that may never be realized. The accounting creates an illusion of profit while concentrating risk. Management gets paid on *"profits that don't exist."*

Buffett used the phrase "weapons of mass destruction." Munger's version was more pointed: the weapons are not the derivatives themselves — the weapons are the accounting rules that let people pretend derivatives gains are real income.

## V. The 25 Psychological Tendencies Applied to Specific Investment Situations

### 5.1 Tendency #1 (Incentive Super-Response) — The Master Key

**Coca-Cola (positive example):**
In a 1996 speech, Munger analyzed Coca-Cola's dominance through the lens of conditioned reflexes and incentive systems. Coca-Cola built its moat by making the product universally available (availability changes behavior), associating it with positive stimuli, and creating a global incentive structure for bottlers and distributors. This is Tendency #1 working in Coca-Cola's favor.
[First-hand, from "The Art of Stock Picking" / Coca-Cola lecture]

**Subprime Mortgage Crisis (negative example):**
Brokers were paid per loan originated, not per loan that performed. Banks were incentivized to package and sell, not to hold. Consumers were incentivized to buy houses they couldn't afford. *"Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome."*
[First-hand, 2009 Berkshire meeting / Wesco meeting]

### 5.2 Tendency #5 (Avoid-Inconsistency) — Why Investors Don't Sell Losers

**Alibaba (self-application):**
Munger initially doubled down on Alibaba despite deteriorating conditions — from 165K shares to 602K shares within three quarters. Then he reversed, halving the position. His post-mortem acknowledged he "got charmed" — Tendency #2 (Liking) — but the doubling-down phase shows Tendency #5 in action: once publicly committed, the impulse is to maintain consistency.

**Auction Dynamics (general):**
Munger noted that in any auction setting, once a bidder publicly commits, they are driven by:
- Consistency (#5): "I already bid, so I must continue"
- Social proof (#15): "Others are bidding, so the price must be reasonable"
- Reciprocity (#9): "I was invited, so I should participate"

### 5.3 Tendency #11 (Psychological Denial) — Why Companies Die

**Kodak:**
*"Kodak was the dominant imaging company in the world... dominated film... knew more about the chemistry of film than anybody else on Earth, and yet the whole business went to zero."*
[First-hand, Daily Journal 2023]

Munger's diagnosis: Kodak's management couldn't accept that digital photography would destroy their core business. This is Tendency #11 in its purest form — denial of a reality too painful to accept.

**General Motors:**
*"General Motors was the most important company in the world when I was young and wiped out its shareholders."*
[First-hand, Daily Journal 2023]

Same pattern: denial of competitive reality until it was too late.

### 5.4 Tendency #12 (Excessive Self-Regard) — Why Smart Investors Make Dumb Bets

**General Application:**
> *"Above all, never fool yourself, and remember that you are the easiest person to fool."* [First-hand]

Munger applies this to himself: the Alibaba mistake was partly excessive self-regard — trusting his own analysis of the Chinese internet opportunity too much, not questioning whether he truly understood the retail dynamics.

### 5.5 Tendency #14 (Deprival Super-Reaction) — Loss Aversion in Action

**Why Investors Hold Losers Too Long:**
The pain of crystallizing a loss is greater than the pain of holding a declining position. Munger recognizes this explicitly and fights it: he sold Alibaba at a loss, overriding the deprival tendency.

### 5.6 Tendency #15 (Social Proof) — The Herd

**SPAC Craze / Crypto / Dot-com:**
> *"It's like somebody else is trading turds and you decide, I can't be left out."*

Munger sees social proof as the single most dangerous tendency in markets because it scales: the more people who participate, the stronger the signal to join.

### 5.7 Tendency #25 (Lollapalooza) — When Multiple Biases Stack

**The 2008 Financial Crisis:**
Munger's most detailed Lollapalooza analysis:
- Incentive bias (#1) at every level of the mortgage chain
- Social proof (#15) among homebuyers and investors
- Denial (#11) about housing prices
- Overconfidence (#13) in risk models
- Authority bias (#22) — rating agencies said it was AAA

Result: *"A combination of megalomania, stupidity, insanity, and I would say evil."*

**GameStop (2021):**
Social proof (#15) + Deprival super-reaction (#14, for short-sellers) + Excessive self-regard (#12, "I'm smarter than Wall Street") + Internet-amplified social cascades. Munger didn't comment extensively on GameStop specifically, but it is a textbook Lollapalooza by his framework. [Second-hand analysis, applying Munger's framework]

**Open Market Auctions:**
Munger explicitly identified auctions as Lollapalooza zones where reciprocity, consistency, social proof, and commitment all stack to produce irrational outcomes. [First-hand, "Psychology of Human Misjudgment"]

## VI. Inversion Methodology Applied to Actual Companies

### 6.1 The Core Principle

> **"Invert, always invert. Turn a situation or problem upside down. Look at it backwards. What happens if all our plans go wrong? Where don't we want to go, and how do you get there?"**
> — Attributed to Jacobi, applied by Munger throughout his career. [First-hand]

### 6.2 The WWII Meteorology Example

In his 2020 Daily Journal address, Munger described his wartime inversion:

As a meteorologist clearing pilots for flights, he asked: *"Suppose I want to kill a lot of pilots. What would be the easy way to do it?"*

Answer: (1) Get them into cold temperatures that cause icing, or (2) Get them into areas where they run out of fuel before they can land.

By inverting the goal (from "keep pilots safe" to "how would I kill pilots"), the safety procedures became obvious. [First-hand]

### 6.3 Costco: Inversion Applied to Retail

Munger's analysis of Costco implicitly uses inversion: instead of asking "What makes a great retailer?", ask "What makes running a retailer miserable?" Answers:
- Thin margins eroded by competition → Costco locks in low margins as policy (11-15% cap)
- Customer disloyalty → Costco creates membership that is psychologically sticky (90%+ renewal)
- Inventory complexity → Costco limits SKUs to ~4,000 (vs. 30,000+ at Walmart)
- Employee turnover → Costco pays above-market wages and promotes internally

The inversion reveals that Costco systematically eliminates the standard failure modes of retail. [Second-hand analysis using Munger's stated framework]

### 6.4 Investment Inversion: "How Could I Lose Money?"

> When investing, instead of asking "How can I make money?", Munger asks "How could I lose money?" By identifying potential losses, he can take steps to mitigate them.

Applied to any stock analysis:
1. **What would destroy the thesis?** (regulatory change, technology disruption, key-man risk)
2. **What am I assuming that might be wrong?** (growth rate, margin stability, competitive moat duration)
3. **If this goes to zero, what was the cause?** (work backward from failure)
4. **What does the bear case look like, and is it survivable?**

### 6.5 The "Great Financial Scandal" as Inversion

Munger's fictional piece about Quant Tech is itself an act of inversion: instead of asking "How do companies report earnings fairly?", he asked *"If I wanted to create a financial scandal, what would I do?"* The answers revealed:
- Exclude stock options from compensation expense
- Gradually inflate earnings bit by bit each year
- Rely on auditors whose incentives are misaligned
- Use complexity to make detection harder

By inverting, Munger produced a fraud detection checklist years before the actual scandals (Enron, WorldCom) materialized.

## VII. Key Contradictions and Tensions in Munger's Adversarial Thinking

### 7.1 The Wells Fargo Blind Spot

Munger and Buffett held Wells Fargo through its fake-accounts scandal (2016-2018), initially defending management. This is notable because:
- Munger's own framework (#1, Incentive Super-Response) perfectly predicted the scandal: Wells Fargo's branch managers were incentivized to open accounts, not to serve customers.
- Munger's own management evaluation framework (integrity as the third filter) should have flagged the culture.
- The eventual diagnosis (*"too slow to fix"*) is strikingly mild compared to how Munger treated Valeant (*"a sewer"*).

**Possible explanation:** Berkshire was the largest Wells Fargo shareholder. Consistency bias (#5) and Liking tendency (#2, for a long-held position) may have delayed Munger's adversarial instincts. This is a case where his own biases may have overridden his framework.

### 7.2 China Risk Assessment: Selective Application

Munger praised China's pragmatism in banning crypto and admired BYD's manufacturing. But he underestimated Chinese regulatory risk when it came to Alibaba and Ant Financial. His framework says "follow the incentives" — but the CCP's incentive structure (political control > market efficiency) applied equally to BYD and Alibaba. The difference in outcome was partly luck.

### 7.3 The "Too Hard" Pile vs. Missing Opportunities

By placing tech in the "too hard" pile for decades, Munger and Buffett missed:
- Google (Berkshire was offered pre-IPO shares by a friend)
- Amazon (Buffett has called missing Amazon a mistake)
- Microsoft (despite Buffett's close friendship with Gates)

The inversion framework would say: "What if our 'too hard' classification is itself a bias?" Munger's answer would be: "The opportunity cost of the ones we missed is real, but less than the cost of the ones we would have lost money on if we'd been less disciplined." Whether this is wisdom or rationalization is genuinely ambiguous.

## VIII. Synthesis: Munger's Adversarial Operating System for Investment Decisions

### The Five-Step Adversarial Checklist (Extracted from Practice)

1. **Invert the thesis.** Before analyzing why an investment will work, list every way it could fail. If you can't articulate the bear case better than the bears, you're not ready.

2. **Check the incentive chain.** Who benefits from this investment working? Who benefits from you investing? If the seller's incentive is to sell — not to own — that's a red flag.

3. **Name the biases.** Explicitly identify which of the 25 tendencies might be distorting your judgment. Am I buying because of social proof? Am I holding because of consistency? Am I attracted to this because of the narrative (Liking tendency)?

4. **Pattern-match to historical precedents.** Every "new" scheme has an old analogue. SPACs → 1960s conglomerates. Crypto → tulip mania. "This time is different" is almost always wrong.

5. **Check for Lollapalooza.** If multiple biases are pointing in the same direction, the risk of extreme misjudgment is non-linear. When everyone around you is excited and the incentives are aligned to promote the asset, that's when you should be most skeptical.
